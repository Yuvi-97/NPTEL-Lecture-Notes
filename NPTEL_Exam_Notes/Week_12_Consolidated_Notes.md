## 1. Sustainable Supply Chain Management (SSCM) (Lecture 58)

### 1.1 The Brundtland Definition & The Triple Bottom Line (TBL)
**Sustainable Supply Chain Management (SSCM)** is the strategic, transparent integration and achievement of an organization’s social, environmental, and economic goals in the systemic coordination of key inter-organizational business processes for improving the long-term economic performance of the individual company and its supply chains.

- **Foundational Definition (UN Brundtland Commission, 1987):**
  > *"Sustainable development is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs."*

- **The Triple Bottom Line Framework (TBL):**
  A sustainable enterprise must deliver value simultaneously across three interdependent dimensions:
  1. **Planet (Environmental Stewardship):** Minimizing ecological degradation, greenhouse gas ($GHG$) emissions, freshwater depletion, toxic effluent discharge, and non-recyclable landfill waste across extraction, production, warehousing, and transportation.
  2. **People (Social Responsibility):** Protecting human rights, ensuring living wages, enforcing strict workplace ergonomics and physical safety, eliminating forced/child labor across multi-tier supplier tiers, and investing in local community education and health.
  3. **Profit (Economic Viability):** Maintaining continuous financial health, positive Return on Investment ($ROI$), positive Economic Value Added ($EVA$), and sustained cash flows. **Critical principle:** *An initiative that fails financially is not sustainable, regardless of its environmental virtues.*

```
                 [ PLANET (Environmental) ]
                Ecological Resource Health
                        ▲       ▲
                       /         \
                      /   SUSTAIN- \
                     /    ABILITY   \
                    ▼     NEXUS      ▼
        [ PEOPLE (Social) ] ◄───► [ PROFIT (Economic) ]
       Human Welfare & Safety     Commercial Viability
```

- **The Green Consumer Premium:** Empirical market research confirms that environmentally conscious consumers are willing to pay an economic premium of **$2\% \text{ to } 10\%$** for products verified to carry a low carbon footprint and ethical sourcing credentials.

### 1.2 Embedding Sustainability Across the Six Supply Chain Drivers
Sustainability must be operationalized systematically across Chopra & Meindl’s six supply chain drivers:
1. **Facilities:** Constructing LEED-certified green warehouses; installing rooftop photovoltaic solar panels; deploying smart LED lighting and high-efficiency HVAC systems.
2. **Inventory:** Utilizing AI-driven predictive demand planning to avoid overproduction, perishable spoilage, and obsolete scrap accumulation; minimizing energy-intensive cold storage dwell times.
3. **Transportation:** Modal shifting freight from carbon-intensive road trucking to energy-efficient rail and coastal shipping; deploying electric vehicles (EVs) for urban last-mile delivery; optimizing routing to eliminate empty backhauls.
4. **Sourcing:** Establishing mandatory supplier codes of conduct; performing independent audits on Tier-1 and Tier-2 vendors for labor rights and toxic emissions compliance.
5. **Information:** Implementing cloud IoT sensors to calculate, trace, and verify carbon footprints at each manufacturing and transit node; printing verified carbon footprint disclosures on consumer packaging.
6. **Pricing:** Introducing tiered green delivery tariffs (e.g., offering discounts for slower delivery slots dispatched via electric or consolidated transport).

### 1.3 Executive Adoption Dynamics & The *VOICE* Framework
Surveys of global supply chain executives reveal critical strategic dynamics:
- **The Core Dilemma:** 8 out of 10 executives are actively expanding sustainability budgets, but confront steep upfront capital expenditure ($CapEx$) and uncertain short-term payback horizons.
- **Top Adoption Drivers:** Resource Conservation & Direct Cost Reduction ($61\%$), Regulatory Compliance Mandates ($51\%$), Supplier & Partner Pressure ($40\%$), Operational Risk Mitigation ($55\%$).
- **The *VOICE* Strategic Implementation Framework:**
  - **V — Visibility & Traceability:** Auditing multi-tier carbon footprints and raw material provenance from source to consumer.
  - **O — Organizational Commitment:** Embedding sustainability targets directly into executive compensation and board governance.
  - **I — Incentive & Grant Utilization:** Leveraging government renewable energy subsidies, green tax credits, and decarbonization grants.
  - **C — Clear Public Goals:** Publishing transparent, third-party audited ESG (Environmental, Social, Governance) targets and net-zero timelines.
  - **E — Expanded ROI Metrics:** Evaluating investments across brand equity, customer retention, sales growth, and regulatory de-risking rather than narrow short-term payback.

### 1.4 Corporate Case Studies in Sustainability
- **Unilever (Agricultural Water Stewardship):** Co-funded the transition of tomato farmers in Brazil and India from flood irrigation to precision drip irrigation, saving millions of cubic meters of water while boosting agricultural yields.
- **Nike (Closed-Loop Manufacturing):** Operates major facilities on $100\%$ renewable energy; created the "Reuse-A-Shoe" program, transforming end-of-life sneakers into athletic turf and running tracks.
- **Walmart (Facility Energy Decarbonization):** Transitioned thousands of retail supercenters to high-efficiency LED lighting and rooftop solar, slashing millions in operational overhead.
- **Starbucks (Ethical Sourcing):** Implemented C.A.F.E. (Coffee and Farmer Equity) practices, purchasing exclusively from certified growers meeting verified social welfare and environmental criteria.
- **Steel Authority of India Limited (SAIL) / Heavy Metals:** Compensates for localized carbon and mineral extraction impacts by building community hospitals, schools, and clean municipal water systems.

## 2. Emergency Supply Chains & Humanitarian Logistics (Lecture 59)

### 2.1 Nature of Emergency Logistics
An **Emergency Supply Chain (Humanitarian Logistics)** is a specialized operational system responsible for mobilizing, procuring, transporting, and distributing life-saving relief supplies (potable water, ready-to-eat rations, emergency trauma kits, temporary shelter, sanitation) and specialized personnel to disaster-stricken populations.

- **Complex Emergency:** A severe humanitarian crisis characterized by combinations of armed conflict, state failure, mass population displacement, famine, and communicable disease outbreaks requiring joint civil-military and international agency coordination.
- **Commercial Disruption vs. Humanitarian Emergency:**
  - *Commercial Disruption:* Involves vendor strikes, machine failure, or localized transit delays where legal corporate identity, financial incentives, and physical infrastructure remain intact.
  - *Humanitarian Emergency:* Catastrophic events (earthquakes, tsunamis, category-5 hurricanes) where road, bridge, electrical, and telecommunication infrastructures are obliterated, creating an operational environment of extreme implied demand uncertainty.
- **The Overriding Humanitarian Mandate:**
  $$\mathbf{\text{Speed and Operational Effectiveness Completely Supersede Cost Efficiency}}$$
  *(Saving human lives within the critical initial response window is the primary measure of operational success; conventional supply chain principles like waiting to consolidate freight for economies of scale are immediately suspended).*

### 2.2 The Four Phases of the Disaster Management Cycle
```
                   [ 1. MITIGATION ]
           Pre-disaster risk analysis, building codes,
                flood barriers, zoning laws
                        │
                        ▼
                  [ 2. PREPAREDNESS ]
       Pre-positioning emergency survival inventory at
           regional hubs, mutual aid agreements
                        │
                        ▼
                    [ DISASTER OCCURS ]
                        │
                        ▼
             [ 3. RESPONSE (First 72 Hours) ]
       Search & rescue, air bridges, trauma medical triage,
              potable water, field hospitals
                        │
                        ▼
                   [ 4. RECOVERY ]
       Long-term rebuilding of infrastructure, housing,
            restoring commercial supply chains
```

1. **Mitigation:** Structural and non-structural interventions executed before a disaster occurs to minimize catastrophic destruction (enforcing seismic building codes, constructing seawalls and levees).
2. **Preparedness:** Pre-positioning emergency relief inventory (water purification kits, tents, medicines) at strategic global and regional staging warehouses (e.g., UN Humanitarian Response Depots); negotiating standby carrier contracts and mutual aid agreements. **Pre-positioning is the single most critical determinant of response velocity**.
3. **Response (Immediate Life-Saving Phase — First 72 Hours):** The critical operational window where immediate search, rescue, trauma surgery, and clean water delivery determine survival rates. Mobilizes emergency air bridges, tactical helicopters, and amphibious transport.
4. **Recovery (Reconstruction & Rehabilitation):** Long-term restoration of transport infrastructure, electrical grids, schools, permanent housing, and local commercial supply networks, spanning months or years.

### 2.3 Four Unique Operational Dimensions
1. **Diverse & Immediate Resource Mobilization:** Concurrently deploying medical trauma specialists, search dogs, heavy earth-moving equipment, water desalination units, and satellite communications.
2. **Extreme Information Scarcity:** Destruction of electrical grids and mobile cell towers renders conventional cloud ERP and GPS systems useless. Needs assessments rely on satellite imagery, drones, and local community scouts.
3. **Effectiveness Over Cost Efficiency:** Chartered cargo airlifts, dedicated helicopters, and less-than-truckload ($LTL$) emergency runs are dispatched immediately without waiting for freight consolidation.
4. **Multi-Stakeholder Inter-Agency Coordination:** Coordinating military logistics units, National Disaster Management Authorities (NDMA), United Nations agencies (UNHCR, WFP, WHO, UNICEF), International Red Cross/Red Crescent, non-governmental organizations (MSF, Oxfam), and commercial corporate donors.

### 2.4 Landmark Humanitarian Case Studies
- **2015 Nepal Earthquake:** Severe Himalayan landslides obliterated mountain highway networks. Relief agencies established a massive **helicopter air bridge**, deploying helicopters to fly emergency trauma kits and food into isolated mountain villages, and evacuating critical casualties on the return leg.
- **2017 Hurricane Maria (Puerto Rico):** Total collapse of the electrical grid rendered hospital diagnostic equipment and vaccine refrigerators useless. Logisticians had to prioritize **diesel generators and mobile telecommunication towers** before pharmaceuticals could be effectively utilized.
- **COVID-19 Pandemic Response:** Mass urban-to-rural migrant movements demanded emergency reallocation of food and healthcare logistics to rural districts; required specialized cryogenic oxygen tankers and sub-zero cold chain logistics for mRNA vaccines.

## 3. Supply Chain Asset Management (Lecture 60)

### 3.1 Foundations and Scope of Asset Management
**Supply Chain Asset Management** is the systematic, lifecycle process of acquiring, deploying, operating, maintaining, upgrading, and cost-effectively decommissioning physical and digital assets utilized directly in the procurement, manufacturing, warehousing, and transportation of goods and services.
- **Scope Boundary:** Encompasses operating facilities, equipment, vehicle fleets, and enterprise software. It **excludes non-operating financial assets** such as liquid cash balances, treasury bonds, or passive corporate equity investments.

### 3.2 Asset Typologies: Tangible vs. Intangible Assets
1. **Tangible (Physical) Assets:**
   - Production plants, automated distribution centers, material handling equipment (MHE: forklifts, automated guided vehicles [AGVs], autonomous mobile robots [AMRs], conveyor networks, crane stackers), freight transport fleets (tractors, semi-trailers, cargo ships, aircraft), and physical inventory (raw materials, WIP, finished goods).
   - Depreciates primarily through mechanical wear, tear, and physical friction.
2. **Intangible (Digital & Relational) Assets:**
   - Enterprise software suites (Enterprise Resource Planning [ERP], Warehouse Management Systems [WMS], Transportation Management Systems [TMS]), algorithmic route-optimization engines, machine learning models, patents, design blueprints, supplier relationship capital, and institutional workforce know-how.
   - Depreciates primarily through functional obsolescence, technological advancement, and security vulnerabilities.

### 3.3 Advanced Tracking & Telemetry Technologies
Modern asset tracking utilizes multi-sensor architectures:
- **Radio-Frequency Identification (RFID) & IoT Sensors:** Passive and active RFID tags provide automated inventory check-in and checkout across automated sorting gates.
- **Fleet Telematics:** Combines GPS positioning, cellular data transmission, and vehicle On-Board Diagnostics ($OBD$) to track location, speed, fuel consumption, driver braking behavior, and engine diagnostics in real time.
- **Thermal Infrared Imaging:** Detects abnormal heat buildup from friction in warehouse conveyor rollers and motor bearings before mechanical seizure occurs; also deployed in disaster relief to detect human body heat in collapsed rubble.
- **Ultrasound Tracking:** Emits high-frequency ultrasonic waves for millimeter-precision indoor positioning of AGVs and forklifts inside steel-reinforced warehouses where satellite GPS signals cannot penetrate.
- **Smart Packaging:** Incorporates printed sensors to detect temperature deviations, package drops, and tampering, automatically notifying WMS systems upon arrival.

### 3.4 The Five Evolutionary Maintenance Strategies
Asset maintenance has progressed through five distinct operational paradigms:

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                   EVOLUTION OF ASSET MAINTENANCE                       │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
 [ 1. REACTIVE ]       Run-to-failure; catastrophic unplanned downtime
        │
        ▼
 [ 2. PREVENTIVE ]     Calendar / mileage-based planned maintenance
        │
        ▼
 [ 3. CONDITION-BASED] Sensor threshold triggered (vibration, heat)
        │
        ▼
 [ 4. PREDICTIVE (PdM)]AI/ML degradation trend forecasting (just-in-time)
        │
        ▼
 [ 5. COGNITIVE ]      Self-learning, autonomous cyber-physical repair
```

1. **Reactive Maintenance (Run-to-Failure):** Equipment is operated until it breaks down. Results in lowest uptime ($< 65\%$), severe secondary mechanical damage, expensive emergency repairs, and catastrophic delivery disruptions.
2. **Scheduled / Preventive Maintenance:** Servicing performed at predetermined fixed calendar or mileage intervals (e.g., servicing trucks every 10,000 km or replacing hydraulic filters every 500 operating hours). Reduces surprise breakdowns but risks replacing components that still possess significant useful life.
3. **Condition-Based Maintenance (CBM):** Servicing is initiated only when real-time sensor parameters (vibration amplitude, operating temperature, oil viscosity) exceed predefined tolerance limits.
4. **Predictive Maintenance (PdM):** Utilizes AI/ML regression models, historical degradation curves, and real-time IoT telemetry to forecast the exact timeframe of failure, scheduling maintenance *just prior* to breakdown. Achieves maximum operational uptime ($> 95\%$) and minimizes total lifecycle maintenance costs.
5. **Cognitive Maintenance:** Autonomous cyber-physical architectures that continuously analyze operating telemetry, dynamically rebalance machine workloads to prevent wear, and independently generate supplier work orders for replacement parts.

### 3.5 Core Asset Productivity Metrics
- **Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE):**
  The global gold-standard benchmark measuring the true productivity of an operational asset:
  $$\mathbf{OEE = \text{Availability} \times \text{Performance} \times \text{Quality}}$$
  Where:
  $$\text{Availability} = \frac{\text{Actual Operating Time}}{\text{Planned Operating Time}}$$
  $$\text{Performance} = \frac{\text{Total Units Produced}}{\text{Theoretical Design Capacity Run-Rate}}$$
  $$\text{Quality} = \frac{\text{Good / Defect-Free Units}}{\text{Total Units Produced}}$$
  - **World-Class Manufacturing Benchmark:** $\mathbf{OEE \ge 85\%}$ (Availability $\ge 90\%$, Performance $\ge 95\%$, Quality $\ge 99.9\%$).
- **Inherent / Operational Availability ($A_i$):**
  $$A_i = \frac{MTBF}{MTBF + MTTR}$$
  - $MTBF$: Mean Time Between Failures (measure of asset reliability).
  - $MTTR$: Mean Time to Repair (measure of asset maintainability).
- **Return on Assets (ROA):**
  $$\text{ROA} = \frac{\text{Net Operating Income}}{\text{Total Operational Assets}}$$

# SECTION B: Important Definitions and Terminology

- **Sustainable Development (UN Brundtland, 1987):** Development meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
- **Triple Bottom Line (TBL):** A corporate framework balancing three performance pillars: Environmental (Planet), Social (People), and Economic (Profit).
- **Green Consumer Premium:** The $2\%-10\%$ price premium environmentally conscious consumers are willing to pay for certified sustainable, low-carbon goods.
- **VOICE Framework:** Five strategic action steps for sustainability: Visibility, Organizational commitment, Incentives, Clear goals, and Expanded ROI.
- **Emergency Supply Chain (Humanitarian Logistics):** A specialized logistics system mobilizing, transporting, and distributing survival supplies and personnel to populations affected by disasters.
- **Complex Emergency:** A severe crisis combining armed conflict, state failure, famine, mass displacement, and epidemics requiring multi-agency coordination.
- **The First 72 Hours:** The critical life-saving window in disaster response during which emergency search, rescue, trauma surgery, and clean water delivery determine casualty survival rates.
- **Disaster Management Cycle:** The continuous four-phase disaster lifecycle: Mitigation $\rightarrow$ Preparedness $\rightarrow$ Response $\rightarrow$ Recovery.
- **Pre-positioning:** Staging emergency relief supplies in strategic regional humanitarian warehouses during the Preparedness phase to guarantee rapid deployment.
- **Air Bridge:** An aerial transport corridor deploying cargo aircraft and helicopters to deliver relief cargo and evacuate casualties when ground roads are impassable.
- **Supply Chain Asset Management:** The lifecycle process of acquiring, operating, maintaining, upgrading, and decommissioning physical and digital supply chain assets (excluding non-operating cash/securities).
- **Tangible Assets:** Physical capital resources (factories, warehouses, material handling equipment, transport fleets, physical inventory).
- **Intangible Assets:** Non-physical digital and intellectual assets (ERP/WMS/TMS software, routing algorithms, patents, supplier trust, brand equity).
- **Telematics:** Integrated vehicular systems combining GPS location, cellular communications, and On-Board Diagnostics (OBD) for real-time fleet tracking.
- **Ultrasound Tracking:** High-frequency acoustic tracking technology used for indoor positioning of AGVs/forklifts in GPS-denied steel warehouse environments.
- **Thermal Imaging:** Infrared camera technology used to detect abnormal mechanical friction in conveyors/motors and locate survivors in disaster debris.
- **Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE):** Quantitative asset efficiency metric calculated as $\text{Availability} \times \text{Performance} \times \text{Quality}$ (World-class benchmark $\ge 85\%$).
- **Mean Time Between Failures (MTBF):** The average operational operating time elapsed between inherent asset equipment breakdowns.
- **Mean Time to Repair (MTTR):** The average time required to troubleshoot, repair, and restore a failed asset to full operational capacity.
- **Predictive Maintenance (PdM):** Maintenance triggered by AI/ML algorithms forecasting impending mechanical failure using real-time IoT sensor telemetry.

# SECTION E: Worked Examples and Applications

### Worked Example 1: Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE) Calculation
**Scenario:** An automated packaging line in a major distribution center operates on a single 8-hour shift (480 minutes).
- Planned downtime includes a 30-minute lunch break and two 15-minute scheduled maintenance checks ($480 - 60 = 420\text{ minutes}$ planned operating time).
- During the shift, the packaging line experienced 42 minutes of unplanned downtime due to conveyor jams and sensor resets ($\text{Operating Time} = 420 - 42 = 378\text{ minutes}$).
- The packaging line is rated at an ideal design speed of **40 parcels per minute**.
- During the shift, the machine packaged a total of **13,608 parcels**.
- Post-packaging quality inspection rejected **272 defective/damaged parcels**.
- **Calculate:** Availability, Performance, Quality, and Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE). Compare against the world-class benchmark ($85\%$).

**Step-by-Step Solution:**
1. **Availability ($A$):**
   $$\text{Planned Operating Time} = 480 - (30 + 15 + 15) = 420\text{ minutes}$$
   $$\text{Actual Operating Time} = 420 - 42 = 378\text{ minutes}$$
   $$\text{Availability} = \frac{378}{420} = \mathbf{0.9000 \ (90.00\%)}$$
2. **Performance ($P$):**
   $$\text{Theoretical Output} = \text{Actual Operating Time} \times \text{Design Speed} = 378\text{ minutes} \times 40\text{ parcels/min} = 15,120\text{ parcels}$$
   $$\text{Performance} = \frac{\text{Total Units Produced}}{\text{Theoretical Output}} = \frac{13,608}{15,120} = \mathbf{0.9000 \ (90.00\%)}$$
3. **Quality ($Q$):**
   $$\text{Good Units Produced} = 13,608 - 272 = 13,336\text{ parcels}$$
   $$\text{Quality} = \frac{\text{Good Units}}{\text{Total Units Produced}} = \frac{13,336}{13,608} = \mathbf{0.9800 \ (98.00\%)}$$
4. **Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE):**
   $$\text{OEE} = \text{Availability} \times \text{Performance} \times \text{Quality}$$
   $$\text{OEE} = 0.90 \times 0.90 \times 0.98 = \mathbf{0.7938 \ (79.38\%)}$$
5. **Managerial Evaluation:**
   The facility achieved an OEE of $79.38\%$. While respectable, it falls short of the **World-Class Manufacturing Benchmark of $85\%$**. The primary performance bottlenecks are machine jams (reducing Availability to $90\%$) and below-rated operating speed (reducing Performance to $90\%$).

### Worked Example 2: Inherent Availability ($A_i$) of a Freight Truck Fleet
**Scenario:** A regional 3PL logistics provider operates a fleet of long-haul freight trucks. Maintenance logs indicate:
- Mean Time Between Failures ($MTBF$) is **180 operating hours**.
- Mean Time to Repair ($MTTR$) for unscheduled breakdowns is **12 hours**.
- Management is evaluating an investment in Predictive Maintenance (PdM) telematics that would increase $MTBF$ to **360 operating hours** and reduce $MTTR$ to **8 hours**.
- Compute the fleet's baseline Inherent Availability ($A_{i, \text{base}}$) and the projected Inherent Availability under PdM ($A_{i, \text{PdM}}$).

**Solution:**
1. **Baseline Availability ($A_{i, \text{base}}$):**
   $$A_{i, \text{base}} = \frac{MTBF}{MTBF + MTTR} = \frac{180}{180 + 12} = \frac{180}{192} = \mathbf{0.9375 \ (93.75\%)}$$
2. **Projected Availability under PdM ($A_{i, \text{PdM}}$):**
   $$A_{i, \text{PdM}} = \frac{MTBF}{MTBF + MTTR} = \frac{360}{360 + 8} = \frac{360}{368} \approx \mathbf{0.9783 \ (97.83\%)}$$
3. **Operational Impact:** The PdM telematics system elevates operational asset availability from $93.75\%$ to $97.83\%$ (a net gain of $4.08\%$ in fleet uptime), drastically reducing delivery delays and expanding fleet freight-carrying capacity.

### Worked Example 3: Humanitarian Emergency Air-Bridge Capacity Planning
**Scenario:** Following a severe earthquake, 60,000 survivors in an isolated mountain district are cut off from all road access.
- International disaster relief protocols mandate supplying **3 liters of potable water** and **1.5 kg of food rations** per person per day.
- A fleet of relief helicopters operates from an emergency regional air bridge.
- Each helicopter has a maximum payload capacity of **3.5 metric tons (3,500 kg)** per flight.
- Because of mountain turnaround and refueling constraints, each helicopter can perform a maximum of **4 round-trip relief flights per day**.
- How many helicopters must be mobilized to satisfy 100% of the daily survival needs of the population?

**Solution:**
1. **Daily Survival Requirement per Person:**
   $$\text{Water: } 3\text{ liters} = 3\text{ kg} \quad (\text{since } 1\text{ liter of water} \approx 1\text{ kg})$$
   $$\text{Food: } 1.5\text{ kg}$$
   $$\text{Total Daily Relief per Person} = 3 + 1.5 = 4.5\text{ kg/person/day}$$
2. **Total District Daily Relief Requirement:**
   $$\text{Total Daily Weight} = 60,000\text{ persons} \times 4.5\text{ kg} = 270,000\text{ kg} = \mathbf{270\text{ metric tons/day}}$$
3. **Daily Carrying Capacity per Helicopter:**
   $$\text{Daily Capacity per Helicopter} = 3.5\text{ tons/flight} \times 4\text{ flights/day} = \mathbf{14\text{ tons/helicopter/day}}$$
4. **Number of Helicopters Required:**
   $$\text{Helicopters Needed} = \frac{270\text{ tons}}{14\text{ tons/helicopter}} \approx 19.29 \implies \mathbf{20\text{ helicopters}}$$
5. **Operational Conclusion:** The disaster management authority must deploy a minimum fleet of **20 helicopters** operating at full operational capacity to prevent acute dehydration and starvation.

8. **Pre-positioning:** Pre-positioning survival inventory at strategic regional hubs during the **Preparedness phase** is the single most critical factor in achieving rapid disaster response.
9. **Asset Management Scope:** Supply chain asset management specifically covers operating facilities, equipment, fleets, and enterprise software; it **excludes non-operating liquid cash and marketable securities**.
10. **Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE):** Formula is strictly multiplicative: **$\text{OEE} = \text{Availability} \times \text{Performance} \times \text{Quality}$**. The world-class manufacturing benchmark standard is **$\ge 85\%$**.
11. **Inherent Availability Formula:** $A_i = \frac{MTBF}{MTBF + MTTR}$.
12. **Predictive Maintenance (PdM):** PdM uses AI/ML degradation modeling and real-time IoT sensors to service machinery *just before* breakdown, achieving the highest operational uptime ($>95\%$).
13. **Indoor Positioning Technologies:** Where satellite GPS cannot penetrate (inside steel warehouses), indoor tracking relies on **ultrasound positioning** and **high-frequency RFID/IoT beacons**.

# SECTION G: Common Mistakes and Tricky Concepts

- **Assuming Sustainability Equals Environmental Preservation Only:** A classic exam mistake. Environmental action ("Planet") is only one of the three pillars of the Triple Bottom Line. An initiative that protects the environment but bankrupts the company ("Profit") or exploits workers ("People") fails the definition of sustainability.
- **Applying Commercial Lean Inventory to Emergency Logistics:** Applying zero-inventory JIT principles to disaster relief is catastrophic. Emergency supply chains require **strategic redundancy and pre-positioned buffer stocks** because implied demand uncertainty is at its maximum.
- **Confusing Disaster Mitigation with Preparedness:** Mitigation involves long-term physical prevention (building earthquake-resistant structures, flood levees); Preparedness involves operational readiness (pre-positioning food/water supplies, training rescue teams, establishing mutual aid contracts).
- **Summing OEE Components Instead of Multiplying:** Students often mistakenly calculate OEE as the average of Availability, Performance, and Quality: $(A + P + Q) / 3$. OEE is **strictly a multiplicative product**: $A \times P \times Q$. If any single parameter collapses to zero, overall productivity is zero!
- **Conflating CBM with Predictive Maintenance (PdM):** Condition-Based Maintenance (CBM) triggers maintenance when a sensor parameter **breaches a static threshold** (e.g., temperature $> 90^\circ\text{C}$). Predictive Maintenance (PdM) is proactive: it uses **AI/ML historical regression models** to project degradation trends and schedule repairs *before* the threshold is breached.
- **Including Liquid Cash in Supply Chain Asset Management:** Liquid cash, bank balances, and investment stocks are corporate financial assets, *not* supply chain operating assets. Supply chain asset management focuses strictly on physical capital (plants, MHE, transport fleets) and digital infrastructure (WMS, TMS, ERP).
