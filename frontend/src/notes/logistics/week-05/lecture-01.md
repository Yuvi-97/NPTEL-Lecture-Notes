# Week 5 – Lecture 1: Fundamentals of Supply Chain Management (SCM)

## 1. Key Concepts

- **Supply Chain Management (SCM):** The systemic, strategic coordination of traditional business functions and tactics both within a particular enterprise and across multi-tier businesses in the supply chain to improve the long-term performance of the individual companies and the network as a whole.
- **The Three Foundational Flows of SCM:**
  1. *Material / Product Flow:* The physical downstream movement of raw materials, parts, work-in-process ($WIP$), and finished goods ($FG$) toward the consumer, as well as upstream reverse flows (returns, repairs, recycling).
  2. *Information Flow:* The bidirectional transmission of demand orders, point-of-sale ($POS$) telemetry, inventory visibility, shipping status, and production schedules.
  3. *Financial / Fund Flow:* The upstream flow of customer payments, credit terms, invoices, debit notes, and warranty settlements.
- **Supply Chain Surplus (Network Value):** The total economic value generated by a supply chain, defined as the difference between the total revenue collected from the end customer and the aggregate supply chain costs incurred across all stages:
  $$\text{Supply Chain Surplus} = \text{Revenue from Customer} - \text{Total Supply Chain Costs}$$
- **Multi-Tier Network Structure:** The structural map of upstream tiers (Tier-2 component makers $\rightarrow$ Tier-1 sub-assemblers) to the focal manufacturer, through downstream distribution tiers (distributors $\rightarrow$ wholesalers $\rightarrow$ retailers) to the end customer.

## 2. Important Points

### Historical Evolution of Supply Chain Management
The lecture traces how corporate operations transitioned across three eras:
1. **1960s–1970s (Functional Fragmentation):**
   - Individual departments operated as isolated, adversarial silos.
   - Purchasing aimed solely at lowest unit purchase price, manufacturing aimed at long production runs to absorb overhead, and warehousing aimed at hoarding stock.
2. **1980s (Internal Integration / Logistics):**
   - Corporations realized siloed optimization led to massive inventory stockpiles and sluggish customer response.
   - Integrated internal functions into two broad umbrellas: *Materials Management* (inbound) and *Physical Distribution* (outbound).
3. **1990s–Present (Extended Enterprise / Modern SCM):**
   - Driven by globalization, rapid IT connectivity (ERP, EDI, cloud platforms), and intense competition.
   - Competition shifted from *brand vs. brand* or *company vs. company* to **supply chain vs. supply chain**.

### The Three Foundational Flows in Action

```
[ Tier-2 / Tier-1 Suppliers ] ◄════════════════════════════════════► [ Manufacturers ]
            │                                                               │
            ▼ (Physical Material Flow: Downstream)                          ▼
[ Inbound Materials ] ──────────────────────────────────────────► [ Assembly / Finished Goods ]
            ▲                                                               ▲
            │ (Information Flow: Bidirectional Demand & Status)             │
[ Point-of-Sale Signals ] ◄───────────────────────────────────── [ Distributor / Retailer ]
            ▲                                                               ▲
            │ (Financial Flow: Upstream Payments & Credit)                  │
[ Customer Payment / Cash / UPI ] ◄────────────────────────────── [ End Consumer ]
```

### Strategic Objective: Maximizing Supply Chain Surplus
- In traditional transactional commerce, buyers and suppliers engage in zero-sum adversarial negotiations (a buyer bargaining down a supplier's price merely shifts profit from one node to another without creating network value).
- Modern SCM aligns incentives across all tiers to eliminate wasteful touchpoints, reduce buffer inventories, and expand the total **Supply Chain Surplus**, sharing the resultant gains equitably across the network.

## 3. Differences / Examples

### Traditional Fragmented Operations vs. Integrated SCM

| Operational Parameter | Traditional Fragmented View | Integrated Supply Chain Management |
|---|---|---|
| **Inventory Strategy** | Independent buffer hoarding at each node. | Centralized visibility; JIT / continuous replenishment. |
| **Cost Focus** | Minimizing local functional costs (transport or purchasing). | **Minimizing Total System Cost**; maximizing total surplus. |
| **Information Sharing** | Proprietary, secretive, distorted by batching. | Open, transparent, real-time shared demand data. |
| **Supplier Relationship**| Short-term, adversarial, multi-bidding. | Long-term, strategic partnerships, joint development. |
| **Primary Metric** | Departmental budget compliance. | Customer satisfaction, OTIF fulfillment, return on capital. |

### Worked Example from the Lecture: Supply Chain Surplus Dynamics
- A customer purchases a high-end smartphone for **\$1,000**.
  - Sourcing components across Tier-1/2 suppliers costs **\$400**.
  - Assembly and testing at the focal plant costs **\$150**.
  - Inbound and outbound logistics across the network costs **\$100**.
  - Retail distribution and merchandising costs **\$100**.
- **Total System Cost** $= 400 + 150 + 100 + 100 = \text{USD } 750$.
- **Supply Chain Surplus** $= 1,000 - 750 = \text{USD } 250$.
- Under modern SCM, this \$250 surplus is shared among suppliers, manufacturer, and distributor. If poor logistics causes a \$150 transit delay, total surplus shrinks to \$100, threatening the financial health of all nodes.

## 4. Must Remember

- SCM coordinates three essential flows: **Material/Physical Flow (downstream & reverse), Information Flow (bidirectional), and Financial/Fund Flow (upstream)**.
- **Supply Chain Surplus** is the ultimate metric of network success:
  $$\text{Supply Chain Surplus} = \text{Customer Revenue} - \text{Total Supply Chain Costs}$$
- Competition in the modern global economy is no longer firm-versus-firm, but **entire supply chain versus entire supply chain**.
- Historical evolution progressed through three phases: **Functional Fragmentation (1960s–70s) $\rightarrow$ Internal Logistics Integration (1980s) $\rightarrow$ Extended Enterprise SCM (1990s–Present)**.
- Adversarial price bargaining does not increase surplus; it merely shifts profit between nodes. Effective SCM **expands the total surplus pie by eliminating network waste**.
- Upstream nodes represent **Tier-1 and Tier-2 suppliers**, while downstream nodes encompass **distributors, wholesalers, retailers, and end consumers**.
