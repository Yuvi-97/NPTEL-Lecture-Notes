# Week 4 – Lecture 3: Logistics vs SCM

## 1. Key Concepts

- **Logistics Management:** The operational component of the supply chain responsible for planning, implementing, and controlling the efficient forward and reverse flow and storage of goods, services, and related information between origin and consumption.
- **Supply Chain Management (SCM):** The overarching strategic integration of all business processes—including product design, strategic sourcing, manufacturing operations, marketing, financial flows, cross-enterprise collaboration, and customer service—across multi-tier suppliers, manufacturers, distributors, and end customers.
- **The Unionist / Inclusion Perspective:** The dominant modern academic and industrial consensus (endorsed by the Council of Supply Chain Management Professionals - CSCMP, Sunil Chopra, Simchi-Levi, and Michigan State University) affirming that **Logistics is a specialized, execution-oriented functional subset of SCM**.
- **Supply Chain Surplus:** The total value generated by a supply chain (the revenue collected from the end customer minus the aggregate costs incurred across all tiers to manufacture and deliver the product). SCM strives to maximize total surplus rather than minimizing costs in one isolated node.
- **Sub-Optimization:** An operational failure where an individual department (e.g., logistics) optimizes its own performance in isolation (e.g., cutting transportation spend by delaying shipments to build full truckloads), which inadvertently creates catastrophic costs downstream (e.g., factory downtime, customer cancellations).

## 2. Important Points

### Four Theoretical Frameworks of the Logistics vs. SCM Relationship
The lecture outlines the historical and conceptual perspectives debates:

1. **Traditional View:**
   - Regards logistics as the overarching, wide-scope discipline, with supply chain management viewed as a narrow subset handling external procurement. *(Now recognized as obsolete).*
2. **Re-Labeling / Equivalence View (Wayne and Johnson):**
   - Asserts there is no substantive operational difference; SCM is simply a modern contemporary buzzword that re-labeled traditional logistics.
3. **The Unionist View (Modern Dominant Paradigm):**
   - Posits that SCM completely incorporates and subsumes logistics. Logistics functions as the physical fulfillment and material-handling engine within broader SCM strategy.
4. **The Intersectional View:**
   - Suggests that logistics and SCM overlap significantly in material management and information flows, but each retains distinct, non-overlapping functions (e.g., SCM handles corporate strategy and supplier relationship contracts; logistics handles deep technical packaging and freight transport mechanics).

### Total System View vs. Siloed Logistics
- **The Systemic Perspective:** An end customer pays a single consolidated price for a product. That revenue must cover the profit margins and operating costs of suppliers, component fabricators, assemblers, transporters, and retailers.
- If logistics acts in a functional silo to minimize freight expenditure, it may choose slower ocean freight over air/rail, causing stockouts that destroy retail sales. SCM enforces a total-system approach where higher logistics costs are justified if they maximize overall supply chain profitability.

### Inbound vs. Outbound Logistics Trade-Offs
The spatial layout and geographic location of manufacturing facilities depend heavily on transportation cost dynamics:
- **Inbound Logistics:** The transportation and storage of raw materials, parts, and sub-assemblies flowing from suppliers into the production facility.
- **Outbound Logistics:** The transportation, warehousing, and distribution of finished goods flowing from the assembly plant to distributors, retailers, and end customers.
- **Weight / Volume Transformation Dynamics (Automotive Case):**
  - Raw materials and automotive stampings can be packed with extreme density: 10 standard shipping containers can easily carry the raw sheet metal and dense components needed to build over 200 automobiles.
  - However, once assembled into 200 fully built finished cars, shipping them requires vastly more cubic trailer volume (multiple specialized multi-tier car-carrier trailers), drastically multiplying outbound freight costs per mile.
  - *Location Decision:* Assembly facilities are strategically sited closer to high-density customer consumption centers rather than remote raw material mines to minimize high outbound transport expenses.

## 3. Differences / Examples

### Comprehensive Comparison: Logistics vs. Supply Chain Management

| Dimension | Logistics Management | Supply Chain Management (SCM) |
|---|---|---|
| **Scope** | Operational and execution-focused. | Strategic, holistic, and cross-enterprise. |
| **Organizational Reach**| Primarily internal to the enterprise or point-to-point. | Multi-tier network spanning suppliers, manufacturers, and retailers. |
| **Core Functions** | Transportation, warehousing, inventory, and order picking. | Product design, sourcing, production planning, finance, and marketing. |
| **Objective** | Achieve the 7 Rights at minimum operational cost. | Maximize total supply chain surplus and competitive advantage. |
| **Inter-Firm Collaboration**| Transactional freight contracts with carriers and 3PLs. | Strategic partnerships, joint forecasting, and shared risk/reward. |
| **Relationship** | **A core functional subset of SCM.** | **The overarching multi-enterprise integration framework.** |

### Industrial Example from the Lecture: Geodis Multi-Client Fulfillment

  - Geodis pools volume across dozens of merchants, providing enterprise-grade demand forecasting, unified inventory tracking, packaging postponement, and next-day carrier dispatch, allowing small brands to achieve large-enterprise supply chain efficiency.

## 4. Must Remember

- The dominant consensus in modern operations is the **Unionist View**: **Logistics is an operational subset of Supply Chain Management**.
- **Logistics manages the physical flow and storage** of goods, whereas **SCM manages end-to-end strategic relationships, product development, procurement, manufacturing, and financial flows**.
- SCM operates under a **Total System Cost perspective**, striving to maximize **Supply Chain Surplus** rather than minimizing individual logistics costs in isolation.
- **Sub-optimization** occurs when minimizing logistics costs (e.g., choosing slow transit) causes larger losses elsewhere (e.g., stockouts, lost customer sales).
- **Inbound logistics** moves raw materials from suppliers to the plant; **outbound logistics** moves finished goods from the plant to consumers.
- Finished goods (e.g., assembled cars) occupy vastly more cubic volume than dense raw components, making **outbound logistics significantly more expensive per unit** and driving assembly plants closer to consumer markets.
- Third-party orchestrators like **Geodis** aggregate logistics volume for small businesses, giving them enterprise-scale supply chain capabilities.
